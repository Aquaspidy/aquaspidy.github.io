R Code for Stats & Graphics

Turning box scores into meaningful visuals — even if you don't know how to code.

Box scores can only tell you so much. Yet you can turn that spreadsheet into valuable insights if you know a few tricks. You can calculate things like offensive and defensive efficiencies, points per possession, and a lot more.

You Don't Need to Be an Expert

I am no expert at R code. There are plenty of resources online to learn how to code in R, but here's the good news: you don't even have to know R code to calculate these stats and generate cool graphs!

Simply go download R Studio and learn the basics on YouTube or GitHub. Then, with AI technology nowadays, you can always ask an AI model that you use all the time to help you write the code.

What to Feed the AI

But what you feed the AI is important. You will need:

  • The box score spreadsheets
  • Most importantly — the game flow / play-by-play chart

As you click buttons during the game, possessions will be recorded. You can clearly see, in order, who grabbed a rebound, who had a turnover, and what happened next.

What About Timestamps?

If you have the financial ability, advanced data recording apps like Synergy will mark the specific time each event happened. But if you use free apps like GameChanger, you won't be able to see the time.

But that's fine! Go to the play-by-play page, take a long screenshot or export the page as a PDF, and feed it to AI — or summarize it yourself. Then ask AI to turn that into charts such as:

  • Lineup efficiency
  • Four Factors (eFG%, TOV%, OREB%, FT rate)
  • Points per possession (PPP)

⚠️ Critical Warning

Stats such as clutch time performance (though you can define clutch time as the last X possessions), timeout efficiencies (how a team performs in the minutes before and after a timeout), or pace control (average length of each possession) CANNOT be calculated if you don't have timestamps.

Don't calculate those — otherwise you'll be feeding false data to your team. Analytics is all about accuracy. Better to have no data than false data.

The Most Important Lesson

However, it is important to note that you don't want to just generate all the data you can. Analytics is not about flooding people's minds with numbers. It's about selecting the meaningful ones and building a comprehensive argument.

You don't want to just present strengths and ignore weaknesses. A player scoring a triple-double without looking at their efficiency is pointless.

Start with a Goal, Not Numbers

This is extremely critical: you need a goal or argument, and this comes from yours and your coach's observations.

For example, your coach might say: "We ain't shooting good enough. Our opponents hit more threes at higher efficiencies than us."

This is where stats come into use. You might want to rewatch game film and pay attention to the shot environment of your team's shooters.

The Problem-Solving Process

🔍 If they are mostly open shots but not hitting them:

The solution is clear — make them hit the gym and practice more.

🔍 If they are all contested fadeaway threes to beat the shot clock:

You might want to find out why. Maybe threes are never your primary weapon, and shooters only get the ball after an unsuccessful drive. Or maybe passes are going directly to shooters rather than creating open spots through plays designed for three-point shots.

📊 Pay attention to assisted field goal percentage:

If it's high, other people are creating opportunities for them. If it's low, they are mostly creating their own space.

The Power of Statistics

Find the problem. Design a process to find the solution. Use R Studio to help you generate good visuals that figuratively present those arguments. Present that process to your coach so you can convince them to make a change.

That's the power of statistics.
